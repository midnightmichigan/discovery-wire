The Galaxy Mapper That Found a Comet It Wasn't Looking For

By The Discovery Wire

ESA / Euclid / Euclid Consortium / NASA / Pöntinen et al.

The European Space Agency's Euclid space telescope has one of the most demanding day jobs in astronomy: map about two billion galaxies, looking up to 10 billion years back in time, to trace how dark energy has stretched the universe. It is not in the comet business. It deliberately avoids the dusty band of sky along the plane of the planets — exactly where comets and asteroids lurk — and it usually photographs each patch of sky only once.

And yet, tucked inside images taken between September 16 and 24, 2026, a smudge moved.

The smudge was flagged by software written by Mikko Pöntinen, a Ph.D. student at the University of Helsinki working with the Euclid Consortium's Solar System Objects Science Working Group. His program was designed to hunt fast-moving objects in Euclid's images — and it lit up on a "streak" smeared across several frames.

"I was excited to see my software flag a 'streak' smeared across several images — the result of a fast-moving object," Pöntinen said. He predicted where the object should appear in other frames and tracked it down in five separate image sets. Then came the tell: a fuzzy cloud around it, the signature of an active comet shedding dust and gas.

It is now officially C/2026 S1 (Euclid). The comet sits roughly 1.3 billion kilometers from Earth — about the distance between the orbits of Jupiter and Saturn — and it rides an orbit well above the plane of the planets, on a journey that will take thousands of years to complete. Astronomers will refine that period as more observations come in.

Its discovery was a long shot layered on a long shot. "Very few comets are expected to be discovered by Euclid so it was a happy coincidence that the combination of the comet's orbit and Euclid's scanning over those days captured the object in multiple images," said Euclid archive scientist Dr. Bruno Altieri.

There is also a dress rehearsal inside the serendipity. Euclid is ESA's deep-space observatory; Comet Interceptor is its sibling mission, designed to wait in orbit and then chase a pristine comet from the outer solar system. C/2026 S1 isn't a candidate for Interceptor — but the way the two teams coordinated on the discovery is, as Altieri put it, "a real test case for how our two mission teams will coordinate in the future."

"And we are of course happy to have discovered a previously unknown comet for the Solar System community to follow up on," added Euclid project scientist Dr. Valeria Pettorino.

So a telescope that wasn't looking found something nobody had seen. Somewhere out past Jupiter, a visitor from the outer dark is shedding dust and gas into the sunlight — and we only know because a student's software refused to ignore a streak.
