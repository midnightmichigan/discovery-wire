A Giant Planet Was Hiding in Two-Year-Old Webb Data — New Software Just Found It

The planet was in the data the whole time. It took a better way of removing starlight to see it.

Photographing a planet outside our solar system is a little like trying to spot a firefly hovering next to a blazing floodlight. Even bright young giant planets can be 100,000 times fainter than their host stars — and as lead author Rodrigo Ferrer-Chavez of Northwestern University puts it, planets overall are "a million times fainter than their stars."

Why the glare wins

Telescopes like the James Webb Space Telescope use coronagraphs to block a star's light, but some glare always seeps through. For 20 years, astronomers have removed that leftover glare by photographing a different reference star and subtracting its pattern. The problem: no telescope stays perfectly still. Thermal changes can expand or contract a mirror by a picometer, and Webb's cryocooler motors vibrate its mirrors — so the reference pattern never quite matches, and the mismatch can bury a planet.

A digital twin of the telescope

Instead of relying on reference photos alone, the Northwestern team built FLIP — Physics-based Learning for Imaging Planets. FLIP creates a "digital twin" of the telescope that simulates how starlight travels through its mirrors, then fine-tunes millions of parameters until the simulated glare matches the real observation. Subtract that, and the planet underneath can emerge.

"We used the same computational tools that researchers use to train AI models, but we did not actually use AI," said co-author Jason Wang. "Because we already know the physics, we don't need AI to understand what's happening."

What FLIP found

Tested on past Webb observations of HIP 65426, 51 Eridani, and the planet-less TYC 5899, FLIP improved sensitivity for detecting faint planets by 2 to 15 times over conventional processing. In the 51 Eridani data — two years old — it recovered the giant planet 51 Eridani b at better than 5 sigma, at the exact wavelength where carbon dioxide in the planet's atmosphere absorbs light and makes the signal even fainter. Ground telescopes had discovered the planet before, but standard processing of Webb's data could not distinguish it from the glare.

"We showed that we can actually recover a planet that people previously couldn't see," Wang said. "This is real data in a real scenario. It was science left on the table because we didn't have the right tools to analyze the data in the most optimal way."

Why it matters

If software can correct for a telescope's imperfections after the fact, astronomers may not need to build ever-more-perfect hardware to find smaller, colder, fainter worlds — including, one day, targets for NASA's proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory, which aims to photograph Earth-like planets and would otherwise need optical stability 100 to 1,000 times beyond Webb's.

"We know of no planets around other stars that are like those in our own solar system," Ferrer-Chavez said. "It's not that they don't exist. It's that we cannot see them."
