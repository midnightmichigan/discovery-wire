A World Maybe 1,000 Kilometers Wide Was Hiding Beyond Pluto

By The Discovery Wire

Illustration by The Discovery Wire

A World Maybe 1,000 Kilometers Wide Was Hiding Beyond Pluto

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile has barely begun its decade-long survey of the southern sky, and it may already have found a new dwarf planet.

The object, designated 2026 ED138, orbits about 46 astronomical units from the Sun — 46 times Earth's distance — beyond Neptune and within Pluto's range of distances. Based on its brightness, astronomers estimate it is between 600 and 1,500 kilometers across, potentially comparable in size to Ceres, Makemake, and Haumea. Icy bodies that large are expected to be round, which is the key requirement for dwarf planet status.

Found by connecting the dots

Rubin operations team member Matthew Holman of the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian spotted the object on October 13, running commissioning data through search software that hunts for moving objects. His first reaction, he said, was "excited disbelief" — and he immediately set out to check whether he or the code had made a mistake.

It had not. Rubin solar system lead Mario Jurić of the University of Washington found 20 more sightings in the data. In all, Rubin caught the object 30 times over 20 nights between March 4 and July 11, 2026. The team then found it again in archival images from the Dark Energy Camera going back to 2016.

The findings were presented on October 27 at the American Astronomical Society's Division for Planetary Sciences meeting in Spokane, Washington, and the International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center announced the discovery the same day.

A candidate, not yet a dwarf planet

There are five officially recognized dwarf planets: Pluto, Ceres, Eris, Haumea, and Makemake. ED138 would be the sixth — if it is confirmed. Astronomers still need to pin down its true size, likely by timing it as it passes in front of a background star (a stellar occultation) and with infrared observations. Holman is careful: it is still a candidate, and confirmation will take months.

What is already clear is how unusual the find is. ED138 appears to be the brightest object discovered in the solar system in nearly 20 years, and Rubin's six-filter colors show it is distinctly red. Holman also noted it is bright enough that very capable amateur astronomers can find it with their own equipment.

Rubin found ED138 in data taken while the observatory was still commissioning, before its 10-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time formally began in June 2026. The same early data has already produced more than 1,000 newly discovered objects beyond Neptune. If this is what the warm-up act looks like, the main survey is going to be interesting.
