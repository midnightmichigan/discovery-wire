They Heard Hydrogen Clumping 4.5 Billion Years Ago — and the Cosmic Web Showed Up

After a decade of work, the international MeerKLASS Collaboration has reported its first measurement of the large-scale clustering of cosmic hydrogen — direct evidence that neutral hydrogen in the distant universe is distributed along the structure of the cosmic web. The study is published in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society.

Mapping hydrogen beyond individual galaxies

The team worked with data from South Africa's MeerKAT radio telescope, through MeerKAT's Large Area Synoptic Survey (MeerKLASS). The technique is called 21-cm intensity mapping: instead of resolving individual galaxies, it combines the hydrogen emission from many galaxies within large patches of sky, capturing the collective pattern even when no single galaxy can be distinguished. That makes it possible, in principle, to build a huge three-dimensional map of the universe.

For this detection, the team analyzed observations across approximately 250 square degrees of sky — roughly the area of 1,250 full moons. The signal originated about 4.5 billion years ago, when the universe was a little older than 9 billion years.

A whisper at a rock concert

Extracting the signal meant removing astrophysical foreground emission about 10,000 times stronger than the hydrogen signal itself — like trying to make out a whispered conversation from the front row of a roaring rock concert.

The measurement also used a completely new observing mode for MeerKAT. Rather than combining its 64 dishes into a single interferometer, the team operated each dish as an independent telescope, trading sharp angular resolution for very high survey speed. That produced many independent maps that could be cross-compared: real cosmic structure recurs across them, while instrumental effects that differ are suppressed. The final signal stands more than six times above the statistical noise.

A decade of teamwork

"This is a milestone in establishing 21-cm intensity mapping as a novel tool for cosmology," said Professor Mário Santos, principal investigator of the MeerKLASS survey at the University of the Western Cape. "This measurement took a decade of patient work by researchers spread across seven countries and three continents."

Dr. Steven Cunnington of the University of Portsmouth, who led development of the analysis pipeline, noted the result caps a journey that began when he started his Ph.D. over a decade ago.

Why it matters

Galaxies trace the cosmic web imperfectly — we only see where stars formed. Hydrogen is the raw material those galaxies are built from, and it fills the web itself. A reliable way to map hydrogen on the largest scales gives cosmologists a second, independent chart of the universe's structure — one drawn in the most abundant element there is.
