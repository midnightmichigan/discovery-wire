The Early Universe's Galaxies Were Too Bright. A Cosmic Leaf Blower May Explain Why.

By The Discovery Wire

Illustration by The Discovery Wire

The Early Universe's Galaxies Were Too Bright. A Cosmic Leaf Blower May Explain Why.

When astronomers pointed the James Webb Space Telescope at the early universe, they found something odd: an overabundance of bright galaxies. Based on what they knew, galaxies that bright should have been far less common in the few hundred million years after the Big Bang.

A new simulation study offers an answer that is almost homely: the young galaxies were running a leaf blower.

Light that pushes

The ultraviolet light in question is called Lyman-alpha radiation. It is produced in abundance around clusters of young stars, and it interacts with hydrogen in an unusual way. When most kinds of light are absorbed by an atom or a dust grain, the energy comes back out at a different wavelength and the light escapes. But hydrogen that absorbs Lyman-alpha radiation re-emits it at nearly the same wavelength — so nearby hydrogen can absorb it again, and again, and again. That repeated recycling makes Lyman-alpha an unusually powerful source of radiation pressure: light that physically shoves matter.

It also makes the effect brutally hard to simulate, because every one of those interactions has to be modeled. "We've known for a very long time that Lyman-alpha radiation could be important because it carries a lot of force," says lead author Shyam Menon, a research fellow at the Flatiron Institute's Center for Computational Astrophysics. "But we've never actually been able to estimate how important it is in a realistic environment because of how challenging it is to model."

A workaround, and a split verdict

Menon and collaborator Aaron Smith of the University of Texas at Dallas took a shortcut: instead of simulating the radiation recycling directly, they analyzed snapshots from an existing simulation of an early-universe star-forming region and calculated the force Lyman-alpha pressure would have exerted on its gas and dust.

The verdict split by density. In dense regions, the outward push could not beat gravity — gas and dust recoiled but stayed bound to the stellar nursery, so star formation itself was probably not much affected. But in the low-density spaces between stars, where gravity is weaker, the radiation won. "When the density is low enough, the Lyman-alpha radiation can push the gas to really high speeds, launching explosive winds that can shoot material clear out of the galaxy," Menon says.

Clearing the fog

That is where the brightness comes in. If Lyman-alpha pressure sweeps loose gas and dust out of the space between stars, it clears the line of sight between those stars and us. "It can sort of clear the 'fog,' allowing us to see stars in the early universe much more clearly than we would otherwise," Menon says. The galaxies may not be intrinsically more extreme than expected — they may simply be easier to see, because their own starlight blew the curtains open.

The team is careful about the limits: this is an important step, not the final word. Fully understanding the effect will take simulations that build Lyman-alpha radiation, gas flows, and star formation together self-consistently — ideally in three dimensions, which the researchers hope improving computing power and better algorithms will allow in the coming years.

The study appears in The Astrophysical Journal (Nov. 4, 2026).
