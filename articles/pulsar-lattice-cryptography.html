A Code-Breaker's Trick Found a Star Spinning 261 Times a Second

By The Discovery Wire

NASA/JPL-Caltech (artist's concept)

A Code-Breaker's Trick Found a Star Spinning 261 Times a Second

The previous best search missed it — even after the equivalent of 600 years of computing on a standard processor. A new method found it in about 10 hours.

An international team using 15 years of publicly available data from NASA's Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope has discovered PSR J1622-7204, an isolated pulsar spinning 261 times per second. The results were published in Physical Review Letters.

Why pulsars are so hard to find

A pulsar is a rapidly spinning neutron star — the dense remnant of an exploded star — that sweeps beams of radiation past Earth like a lighthouse. In gamma-ray data, Fermi detects only a tiny number of photons from any one pulsar, mixed into background noise. To find the signal, researchers must guess several unknowns at once: spin rate, spin-down, and exact sky position. Searching every combination is computationally impossible by brute force.

The lattice trick

Co-authors Dotan Gazith and Barak Zackay of the Weizmann Institute of Science realized the search could be reframed as a geometry problem: finding the shortest vector in a high-dimensional lattice. That technique draws on algorithms developed by cryptographers working on encryption designed to resist future quantum computers. The team stresses they are not doing cryptography — they are borrowing its mathematics.

Each trial took about five minutes on a single computer core. Once the method flagged a candidate, the full Fermi dataset confirmed a signal too strong to be chance.

What comes next

The team estimates tens or possibly hundreds more pulsars may already sit hidden in the Fermi archive. Their next goal is harder still: pulsars in binary systems, where a companion star's gravity distorts the signal. They plan to combine the new algorithm with the volunteer computing power of Einstein@Home.

All of this was possible because Fermi data is fully public. As Fermi project scientist Elizabeth Hays put it, that openness has made all of the science better.
