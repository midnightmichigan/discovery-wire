Mars Dust Is Kinder to Spacesuits Than Moon Dust — and Sneakier

A rover does not need a spacesuit. But NASA's Perseverance has been wearing small swatches of spacesuit material — each less than a square inch — ever since it landed on Mars. The swatches are a dress rehearsal: a way to learn what Martian dust does to the fabrics future astronauts will depend on, years before any human arrives.

A new study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, led by Scott VanBommel of Washington University in St. Louis, has now read those swatches after 1,645 Martian days of exposure.

The good news first

Lunar dust was brutal on Apollo. It scratched visors, degraded spacesuits, and clung to suits until it contaminated the cabin air and gave astronauts hay fever-like symptoms. Mars dust, the study suggests, may be gentler. The swatches showed little visible wear, and the particles themselves are much smoother than the jagged grains found on the Moon.

"The particles in the Martian atmosphere have been blowing in the wind for potentially billions of years, so they tend to be well rounded, like pebbles in a river," VanBommel said.

The catch: ultrafine and toxic

Mars dust makes up for its smoothness with size — or the lack of it. Some particles are ultrafine specks as small as 100 nanometers across, one ten-thousandth of a millimeter. On Earth, particles that fine, from smoke and air pollution, can penetrate deep into the lungs and enter the bloodstream. Astronauts will not breathe the Martian air directly, but habitats, suits, and air filters will have to be designed for a hazard measured in nanometers.

Chemical analysis from Perseverance's PIXL instrument adds a second warning: unlike lunar dust, the Martian material contains perchlorates and other potentially toxic compounds, and is enriched in sulfur, iron, and silica. Dose and duration decide how much that matters — but mission planners now have to design for it.

Rovers as test pilots

"We've been operating rovers on the surface of Mars continuously for more than 20 years at this point," VanBommel said. "We know that dust will be a challenge, but it's something we can prepare for and overcome."

That is the quiet pattern of Mars exploration so far: every robot is also a test pilot for the humans who have not arrived yet. This time, the test result reads: the dust will not sandpaper your suit — but do not underestimate the finest, toxic edge of it.
