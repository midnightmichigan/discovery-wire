Giant Binoculars, Honest Verdict: The Celestron SkyMaster 15x70

Every time we post a meteor shower or a bright comet, the same question lands in our inbox: what should I actually look through? Telescopes get the glory, but for a first real instrument we keep recommending the same thing we recommended in our Orionids guide — a pair of giant astronomy binoculars. And the pair that has defined the budget end of that category for nearly two decades is the Celestron SkyMaster 15x70 (model 71009).

Full honesty up front: this is a specs-and-track-record guide, not a hands-on lab test. But this model's record is unusually public — it has been sold since 2007, and Celestron bills it as its bestselling astronomy binocular. Here's what the numbers say.

What it is

  • 15x magnification with 70mm objective lenses — the big front glass is the point. Seventy millimeters per eye gathers far more light than a standard 50mm binocular, which is exactly what faint sky objects demand.
  • BaK-4 Porro prisms and multi-coated optics, per Celestron's specifications — the decent-glass checklist at this price tier.
  • A 4.4-degree field of view and 18mm of eye relief (retailer spec sheets) — wide enough to sweep the Milky Way star fields, and workable for eyeglass wearers.
  • In the box: the binocular, a tripod adapter, carrying case, rainguard, objective lens caps, neck strap, and lens cloth.

Through a 15x70, the Moon stops being a disc and starts being terrain. Jupiter's four big moons line up visibly. The Orion Nebula, the Pleiades, and the Andromeda Galaxy — all objects we cover here as naked-eye-or-better targets — turn into actual sights instead of smudges.

What it isn't

  • It is not a handheld binocular, whatever the ads imply. At 15x and about 3.3 pounds, hand shake will smear the fine detail. Celestron itself says this binocular should be mounted on a tripod — the adapter is included, but the tripod is not. If you don't already own a camera tripod, budget for one.
  • It is not a telescope replacement. There is no swapping eyepieces or cranking magnification for planetary close-ups. What you get instead is a wide, bright, two-eyed view — which, for learning the sky, is arguably better.
  • It is not travel glass. It's big. This is a backyard-and-car-camping instrument.

Our verdict

If your budget for a first sky instrument is modest, the honest order of operations is: binoculars first, telescope later. The SkyMaster 15x70 has survived nearly twenty years of buyers, reviews, and forum arguments because the formula works — maximum light grasp per dollar, on a tripod, under a dark sky.
