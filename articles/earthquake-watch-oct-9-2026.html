Weekly Earthquake Watch: A 6.3 off Vanuatu Caps Seven Busy Days

By The Discovery Wire

Illustration by The Discovery Wire

The planet rang its bell 116 times at magnitude 4.5 or greater this week — and the loudest ring almost nobody heard.

Here's the seismic log, straight from the public USGS-tracked feeds for the seven days ending October 8, 2026.

The week's largest: M6.3, Vanuatu

A magnitude 6.3 earthquake struck 102 km northeast of Norsup, Vanuatu, early Thursday morning (about 5:00 AM Eastern). It was shallow — only 10 kilometers deep — and USGS issued a green alert, meaning the chance of fatalities or significant damage was low. Just five people filed "Did You Feel It?" reports, because the epicenter sat in open ocean far from population centers.

Big number, quiet outcome. That's the Pacific Ring of Fire doing what it does: most of the energy of the planet's biggest quakes goes straight into water, mud, and uninhabited islands.

The rest of the M5.5+ club

  • M5.9 — 39 km WSW of Tambolaka, Indonesia (Sat Oct 3, evening). The second-largest of the week, offshore, handful of felt reports.
  • M5.8 — 165 km SSE of Vilyuchinsk, Russia (Fri Oct 2). The Kamchatka Peninsula's long tradition of offshore rumblers.
  • M5.8 — 262 km SSW of Severo-Kuril'sk, Russia (Tue Oct 6). Same week, same region — the Kuril arc was active.
  • M5.5 — 92 km NNW of Aleneva, Alaska (Tue Oct 6). A reminder that the Aleutian chain is always awake.
  • M5.5 — west of Macquarie Island (Thu Oct 8). Nobody lives there. The penguins didn't notice.
  • M5.5 — 17 km SW of Burias, Philippines (Thu Oct 8). Shallow and close to shore, but no damage reports.

No M7+ anywhere on the planet this week. 116 quakes of M4.5 or stronger, zero deaths. That's a good week by seismology's standards.

The instrument note

We are not forecasting here — nobody can. We're just reading the instruments out loud. Seismographs don't sleep, and neither does our interest in them. The pattern this week is the usual one: the planet releases its energy mostly where nobody is standing, in deep ocean trenches and remote arcs. The danger, as always, is the rare one that happens under a city.

The gear desk: one cheap recommendation

Every earthquake week ends with the same thought from us: the information layer that matters most in a disaster isn't a seismometer — it's whatever tells you what to do after the shaking. When the grid goes down, a battery-free emergency weather radio keeps you connected to NOAA alerts, and a hand crank or solar panel means it never needs a wall outlet.

We've added one to our preparedness corner on Facebook — it's genuinely the cheapest insurance a household can own for any disaster, quake or otherwise. No gimmicks: it receives alerts, it charges a phone, and it keeps working when nothing else does.
