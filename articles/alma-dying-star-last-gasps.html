A Star's Last Gasps: ALMA Catches a Dying Star Mid-Transformation

By The Discovery Wire

ALMA (ESO/NAOJ/NRAO); Sahai et al. (2026)

Stars like our Sun don't die quietly. In their final act, they swell into red giants, shed their outer layers, and leave behind a glowing cloud called a planetary nebula. That transformation takes only a few thousand years — a blink in a star's life — so catching one in the act is rare.

Using the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA), a team led by astronomers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory has done just that. Their new images of a star called IRAS 06530-0213, about 12,000 light-years away in the constellation Monoceros, reveal a giant ring of gas that appears to be a frozen fossil of the star's last thermonuclear flare-up, encircling an hourglass-shaped nebula that is only now taking shape.

"Most of us think of stars as unchanging points of light, but this is a real-time snapshot of a star's death throes," said Raghvendra Sahai, senior research scientist at JPL and lead author of the study, published in The Astrophysical Journal.

A heartbeat frozen in gas

Late in life, a star like this burns hydrogen and helium in shells around its core. Every so often, that helium shell flares up in a thermal pulse — a brief burst of nuclear burning that jolts the star and flings material into space. Theorists have long predicted such pulses leave shells of gas behind, but no one had seen one around a star that had already begun its final transformation.

In IRAS 06530, ALMA found a thin, spherical shell of gas that looks like a ring about half a light-year across. Measuring how fast the gas is moving outward, the team estimates the shell was blown off about 6,500 years ago. This is the first direct evidence of the last thermal pulse before a star of this type enters its final phase, the team says.

What came next

After the pulse, the star's mass loss dropped off for roughly 2,000 years. Then, over the next ~1,000 years, it rose sharply again, shedding a thick, lumpy shell now seen as a tangle of curved filaments. Inside that, the star has begun to carve out a bipolar, hourglass-shaped nebula and a dense doughnut of gas around its middle. By the team's estimate, these youngest structures formed within just the last 700 years — meaning the star crossed over from its red-giant phase very recently, in astronomical terms.

The curved filaments appear to form part of a spiral pattern — the kind usually attributed to an unseen companion star stirring the outflowing gas as it orbits.

Two complications

The gas near the star carries an unusual isotopic fingerprint: more than expected of the rare, heavy forms of carbon, oxygen, and nitrogen — a pattern standard models don't explain. And ALMA sees no compact dusty disk around IRAS 06530, even though dying stars with companions typically have one. One possibility: a disk existed briefly, powered a short burst of jets that helped sculpt the hourglass, and then faded away.

The observations are part of PANORAMA, an ALMA survey of dying stars transitioning to planetary nebulae. IRAS 06530 is the first object from the survey to be presented in detail.

Source: National Radio Astronomy Observatory, Nov 20, 2026. Paper: Sahai et al., The Astrophysical Journal (2026), DOI 10.3847/1538-4357/aeab9a.
