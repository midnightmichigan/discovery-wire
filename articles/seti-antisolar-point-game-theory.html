When Should We Listen for Alien Signals? SETI Turns to Game Theory

Finding a signal from another civilization takes more than pointing a telescope in the right direction. We may also need to listen at exactly the right time.

Scientists at the SETI Institute are trying a new approach to that problem: combining astronomy with game theory to predict when another civilization might decide to send a message to Earth. Using the Allen Telescope Array at the Hat Creek Radio Observatory in Northern California, the team has launched a multiyear search focused on one particular location in the sky — the anti-solar point.

The meeting place neither side has to agree on

In game theory, a Schelling point is a choice two parties might independently make when they need to coordinate without communicating. If you agreed to meet a stranger in New York City with no time or place set, both of you might pick noon at Grand Central Terminal — not because you discussed it, but because it is the obvious focal choice.

The same principle could apply across interstellar distances. A civilization that wants to contact Earth could save energy by sending a focused signal at a specific time instead of broadcasting constantly in every direction. But how would it pick a moment when someone on Earth might be listening?

Why the anti-solar point?

From certain locations in the galaxy, an observer could watch Earth pass in front of the Sun — a transit. That event would reveal Earth's existence, its orbit, and its position in the Sun's habitable zone. At the midpoint of that transit, Earth lies directly between the distant observer and the Sun. From Earth's perspective, that transmitter would appear in the direction opposite the Sun: the anti-solar point.

Because both civilizations could independently recognize that alignment, it offers a possible way to coordinate without ever having exchanged a message. The idea does not assume such civilizations exist, or that they would use this strategy. It gives scientists a clear, testable idea about where and when to search.

"The challenge in SETI isn't simply deciding where to point a telescope," said Dr. Sofia Sheikh, SETI Institute research scientist and co-lead author of the paper. "It's figuring out where and when another civilization might choose to transmit. The anti-solar point gives us a way to think about that problem from the perspective of a potential sender, rather than just the receiver."

What the pilot found

Before the larger campaign, the team ran a three-night pilot survey in the summer of 2023. Using 20 antennas of the Allen Telescope Array, researchers collected approximately 17 hours of observations, generating 32 terabytes of data. The first analysis found about 1.9 million radio signal detections. After filtering, the team found no signals that matched extraterrestrial technology — all of them could be explained by radio interference from human sources.

Even so, the pilot was a milestone: the first observational search of the anti-solar point, putting a decades-old proposal to the test for the first time, and establishing the first limits for transmitters using this strategy. Under certain assumptions, the observations were sensitive enough to detect a transmitter comparable in power to the former Arecibo planetary radar at distances up to approximately 124 light-years.

A four-year watch

As Earth moves around the Sun, the anti-solar point shifts against the background stars. The team makes repeated observations all year, planned at two to three nights each week, aiming to cover all the important positions along Earth's orbit over about four years. The campaign has already gathered more than 121 nights of observations since the pilot. Results from the first year of data will be shared in a future publication.

Whether or not the survey ever finds a technosignature, it will set new limits on possible transmissions and show which parts of this search space have actually been checked. Finding another civilization may depend not just on knowing where to look, but on knowing when to listen.
