It Looked Like One of the First Galaxies. Then It Moved.

A faint red object spotted by the James Webb Space Telescope originally appeared to offer a glimpse of the universe's first galaxies. But images taken about 3.5 years apart revealed a giveaway: the object, nicknamed Capotauro, had moved.

That shift places it much closer to home. Capotauro is actually a cold brown dwarf in our Milky Way, rather than a galaxy whose light has traveled across almost the entire history of the universe.

F.-Y. F. Liu of the University of Edinburgh and colleagues report the finding in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society.

How a cold object mimics an ancient galaxy

Capotauro appeared in some of Webb's longer-wavelength infrared images but disappeared at shorter wavelengths. That pattern made it an intriguing candidate for an extremely distant galaxy.

Interpreted as a distant galaxy, its cutoff suggested a redshift of about 32 — roughly 100 million years after the Big Bang, an exceptionally early chapter of galaxy formation.

Yet the original study, published in February in Astronomy and Astrophysics, also considered a very different explanation: a cold object within our own galaxy. Brown dwarfs lack enough mass to sustain hydrogen fusion. The coldest varieties, called Y dwarfs, have atmospheres containing water, methane and ammonia that absorb broad ranges of infrared light, leaving a steep rise in brightness at longer wavelengths that can resemble the distant-galaxy cutoff.

Colors alone could be misleading. Movement offered a different test.

The giveaway was its position

Liu's team compared Webb images from December 2022 with observations taken in June 2026. They aligned the images using 68 compact reference sources, then inserted artificial objects into the real backgrounds to check how noise could distort their position measurements.

Capotauro had shifted by about 0.13 arcseconds — a tiny angle, but a detection with a statistical significance exceeding six sigma. An extremely distant galaxy would appear essentially fixed. An object moving within the Milky Way could show just such a shift.

The best match suggests a temperature of about 350 Kelvin (77°C) and a distance of roughly 2,400 light-years. At that distance, the authors describe Capotauro as one of the most distant Y dwarfs found to date — though it could in fact be colder, fainter and closer.

The reclassification offers a useful lesson for galaxy hunters: Webb can uncover cold objects that imitate the most extreme early-galaxy candidates. When colors leave identification uncertain, returning to the same patch of sky can reveal which objects have moved — and which may really belong to the distant universe.
