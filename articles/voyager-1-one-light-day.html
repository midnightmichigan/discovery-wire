In 17 Days, Light Itself Will Need a Full Day to Reach Our Farthest Machine

We measure the universe in light-time without thinking about it: light-minutes to the planets, light-years to the stars. This month, humanity's farthest machine adds a new unit to the family.

On November 18, 2026, NASA's Voyager 1 will cross the one-light-day mark — 16.1 billion miles from the Sun, 49 years after its launch. From that day on, a radio signal traveling at the speed of light will need a full 24 hours to reach it. A command sent on a Monday morning arrives on Tuesday.

A 1977 road trip that never ended

NASA launched the twin Voyager spacecraft in 1977, taking advantage of a once-every-175-year alignment of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Using six gravity assists between them, the pair completed a Grand Tour of the outer planets, then went their separate ways at Saturn. Voyager 1 took one final portrait of the solar system — including the famous pale blue dot image of Earth — and kept going.

Each craft still carries the Golden Record: a 12-inch phonograph record with spoken greetings in 55 languages, 27 musical tracks, 116 images, and other sounds of Earth, protected by a cover etched with our Sun's location relative to 14 pulsars and plated with uranium-238, whose 4.468-billion-year half-life can date the launch for whoever finds it.

Still phoning home

In August 2012 and November 2018, Voyager 1 and 2 crossed the heliopause, the boundary where the Sun's solar wind meets interstellar space. NASA's Deep Space Network still talks to both. When NASA published its November sky notes on October 30, the Canberra antenna was tracking Voyager 1 at 25.80 billion kilometers — just over 16 billion miles.

Seventeen days from now, that number crosses a threshold no human-made object has ever reached: so far away that light itself needs a day for the trip.
