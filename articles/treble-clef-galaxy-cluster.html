There's a Treble Clef Hanging Over Two Colliding Galaxy Clusters

By The Discovery Wire

Illustration by The Discovery Wire (generated artwork for this story)

There's a treble clef hanging in the sky — and it took two X-ray telescopes to figure out what's holding it up.

Astronomers have been puzzling for years over a formation of radio waves shaped like a musical symbol, nicknamed the "Treble Clef" (catalogued as VLSS J0318.9+5755). The hard part wasn't the shape — it was the location. The formation sits almost directly behind the plane of our own Milky Way, so the stars, gas, and dust of home block the view from Earth.

A team led by Andrea Botteon of Italy's National Institute for Astrophysics (INAF) peered through the veil with instruments that don't care about dust: NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory and the German eROSITA X-ray telescope. Both found diffuse, multimillion-degree gas exactly where the Treble Clef sits — the fingerprint of a galaxy cluster. Closer analysis revealed something bigger: it's not one cluster, but two galaxy clusters in the process of merging, some of the largest structures in the universe.

The two-act explanation

The team proposes the Treble Clef formed in two stages:

Act one — the black hole's crescendo. Long ago, the supermassive black hole at the cluster's center went through an active phase, driving clouds of charged particles and magnetic fields outward at nearly the speed of light. Those clouds blazed in radio waves — then gradually faded as their particles lost energy.

Act two — the collision. A few hundred million years ago, the two clusters collided. The crash compressed and re-energized the old, faded cloud, and the radio emission flared back to life. Shock waves from the collision — analogous, the researchers say, to sonic booms from supersonic jets — may have played a role in the re-energizing.

To confirm the collision, the authors used a special technique: they measured how much the cluster's hot gas deviates from a neat symmetrical oval, then subtracted the oval from the X-ray image. The leftover emission was brightest on the left and right of the cluster's center and faintest in the middle — evidence the collision struck along the left–right axis. The clusters, the team concludes, crashed a few hundred million years ago and are now moving away from each other.

Seeing in three kinds of light

The composite image of the Treble Clef layers three views of the same patch of sky:

  • Blue — multimillion-degree gas seen in X-rays by Chandra and eROSITA
  • Red — the radio formation seen by the LOw Frequency ARray (LOFAR); this is where the "musical notation" doppelganger appears
  • White — infrared light from the United Kingdom Infrared Telescope

Yes, it's pareidolia

Before anyone gets poetic: NASA is clear that the resemblance to a treble clef is pareidolia — the phenomenon of seeing familiar shapes in random data, like faces in clouds. There is no scientific connection to musical notation. The researchers note, however, that a second pareidolia reading is available: the same radio structure could be seen as a dragon, viewed from the side.

"The researchers discovered the Treble Clef is likely caused by an outburst from a supermassive black hole, followed by a collision of two galaxy clusters." — NASA Science, Oct 14, 2026

The paper describing the findings was published October 14, 2026 in Astronomy & Astrophysics (Botteon et al., INAF).
