A Lava World Circles Its Star Every 5.4 Hours — and Hubble Just Watched It Disappear Behind It

Thousands of exoplanets have been found by watching a star dim as a planet crosses in front of it. Far fewer have been caught doing the opposite — slipping behind their star, in what astronomers call a secondary eclipse. That second, subtler dip carries information about the planet itself: its dayside temperature, how reflective its surface is, and whether it holds an atmosphere.

Now, Sebastian Zieba of the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian and collaborators report that the Hubble Space Telescope has detected the secondary eclipse of a rocky exoplanet for the first time — the extreme lava world TOI-2431b.

A 5.4-hour year

TOI-2431b orbits its K-type host star every 5.4 hours, making it the shortest-period planet with a measured mass and radius: about 6.2 Earth masses and 1.54 Earth radii. At that distance, the dayside is expected to be molten — a lava world.

Zieba and coauthors combined Hubble near-infrared observations with visible-light data from NASA's TESS satellite, which first detected the planet in 2019. The eclipse points to a dayside brightness temperature of about 2,524 K — hot enough to melt many types of rock, and roughly what you would expect for a world that absorbs all incoming energy and redistributes none of it. That would make TOI-2431b an outlier among lava worlds studied so far, which usually show some mechanism — a reflective surface, clouds, or efficient heat transport — that cools the dayside.

Atmosphere, or bare rock?

The honest answer is: not yet known. At the wavelengths Hubble and TESS can see, thermal emission from the planet cannot be separated cleanly from starlight reflected off it. The team's models show that a planet with a superheated rock-vapor atmosphere and a planet with no atmosphere at all would look similar in the current data. The observations are consistent with no atmosphere — but they do not rule one out.

That is where the James Webb Space Telescope comes in. JWST observes at wavelengths where a rock-vapor atmosphere, if one exists, could actually be detected. Hubble's first is best read as a proof of capability — and a setup for the follow-up.

Source: AAS Nova (American Astronomical Society), Nov 4, 2026. Paper: Zieba et al. 2026, The Astrophysical Journal Letters.
