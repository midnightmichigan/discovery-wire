A Michigan winter outage is rarely dramatic in the first hour. It gets expensive later: a burst pipe, a fridge full of lost food, a house that drops below freezing while everyone is asleep or at work. Readiness is not a bunker. It is a short list of cheap, boring things done before the storm, so the bad night stays a bad night instead of becoming a repair bill.

Start with water: pipes fail before people do

When a house loses heat, exposed pipes are the first real risk. The fixes are mostly insulation and habit, not equipment.

  • Insulate exposed pipes in basements, crawl spaces, and exterior walls with foam pipe insulation sleeves. They are inexpensive, cut to length with scissors, and need no tools beyond tape at the seams.
  • Know your main shutoff and make sure everyone adult in the house knows it too. A burst pipe with a known shutoff is a mop job; without one, it is a flood.
  • Let faucets drip on the coldest nights when a pipe run is known to freeze. Moving water is harder to freeze, and the small water cost is far cheaper than a plumber's emergency call.
  • Open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls so warm room air reaches the pipes.

A battery water-leak alarm under the water heater and behind the washing machine is the cheapest early warning in the whole house. It does not prevent anything — it buys you the ten minutes that prevent everything else.

Light and information when the power goes

Skip the candle plan. Modern LED lanterns run for days on a set of batteries, are safe around kids and curtains, and light a whole room instead of one corner of it.

  • One lantern per main living space, plus a headlamp per adult. Headlamps are the unglamorous winner: cooking, diaper changes, and breaker-box trips all need two hands.
  • A battery or hand-crank weather radio matters in Michigan specifically because lake-effect storms can take down power and cell signal in the same night. The radio is how you learn whether it is a two-hour outage or a two-day one.
  • Keep one power bank charged for phones. Rotate it monthly; a power bank that sat in a drawer for a year is a paperweight with a cable.

Heat backup: the honest version

There is no safe, cheap way to heat a whole house without its furnace, and any article that implies otherwise is selling something dangerous. What you can do:

  • Shrink the space. In an outage, close doors and live in one or two rooms. Blankets over doorways genuinely help. A family sleeping in one room stays far warmer than the same family spread across four.
  • Layer the beds, not just the people. An extra blanket under the fitted sheet insulates from the cold mattress below, which is where most heat is lost in bed.
  • Never run a generator indoors or in an attached garage, and never heat with a gas oven. Carbon monoxide is the outage injury that actually kills people, and it is silent. A battery CO detector with a digital display is not optional equipment in a house with gas appliances — it is the detector that matters most in winter.

Food and the 48-hour rule

A full fridge keeps food cold roughly four hours unopened; a full freezer, about 48 hours (24 if half full). The readiness move is not buying special food — it is keeping the freezer reasonably full (jugs of frozen water fill gaps and double as ice packs) and owning a cheap fridge thermometer so "is this still good?" is a measurement, not a guess.

The one-page version

Print a single sheet: shutoff locations, utility outage number, lantern and radio locations, and who to call. Ours lives in the Household Emergency Binder, which is also where the insurance and shutoff information sits in normal times — the binder is the boring system that makes the bad night manageable.

The whole readiness list above costs less than one emergency plumber visit. That is the entire argument.
