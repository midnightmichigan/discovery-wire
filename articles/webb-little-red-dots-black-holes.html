Webb's Little Red Dots May Be Black Holes Being Born

By The Discovery Wire

NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI, Dale Kocevski (Colby College)

Webb's Little Red Dots May Be Black Holes Being Born

They are only a few hundred light-years across, but they shine as brightly as much larger galaxies — and nothing in the old theories predicted them.

Since the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) began peering deeper into the cosmos than ever before, astronomers have found hundreds of these compact, bright objects, known as "little red dots" (LRDs). They appear as tiny red specks in the early universe, less than a billion years after the Big Bang, with unusual V-shaped spectra and a puzzling lack of detectable X-ray and radio emissions.

"LRDs have been one of the most surprising discoveries made by the James Webb Space Telescope," said lead author Dr. Daniel Whalen, from the University of Portsmouth's Institute of Cosmology and Gravitation. "They are compact, only a few hundred light-years across, but are as bright as much larger galaxies, and their unusual V-shaped spectra have defied the usual astrophysical explanations."

Black holes hidden inside dense gas

Using advanced cosmological simulations, Whalen's team found that little red dots are most likely primeval galaxies hosting massive black holes formed by the collapse of supermassive primordial stars. Crucially, the black holes remain wrapped inside the enormous disks of gas that created them, hiding much of their activity from view.

The simulations suggest these objects contain black holes with masses around 10 million times that of the Sun. The dense gas surrounding the black hole traps high-energy X-rays — which would explain why Webb sees no X-rays from them — while allowing some ultraviolet, visible and infrared light to escape. That naturally recreates the unusual light signatures JWST observes.

At the same time, a large neighboring cluster of stars in the small galaxy produces the blue portion of the spectrum, helping to explain another long-standing feature of little red dots. The simulations also show dense clusters of around 100 million stars packed into a region only around 500 light-years across, matching what astronomers observe in real LRDs.

A model that fits — not a sighting

Earlier theories suggested the dots could be compact collections of stars, heavily obscured black holes, supermassive stars, or even hypothetical exotic objects such as "black hole stars." None could fully explain their formation, abundance, lifespan and unusual spectral signatures. The new model successfully reproduces the properties of a wide variety of little red dots observed by JWST, including some of the most distant and extreme examples ever discovered.

That is still a model, not a direct observation of a black hole being born — and other proposals remain in play. But the fit is striking.

"What makes this discovery so exciting is that we may be witnessing the birth of the first supermassive black holes," Whalen added. "For decades, simulations have predicted that these objects should exist, and now the James Webb Space Telescope may finally be allowing us to see them."

Source: Phys.org, Nov 10, 2026. Paper: Daniel J. Whalen et al., "Little Red Dots are Direct-collapse Black Hole–forming Galaxies," The Astrophysical Journal Letters (2026), DOI: 10.3847/2041-8213/aeb37a.
