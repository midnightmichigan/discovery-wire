After 42 Years, Physicists Caught a Decay That Happens Once in 10 Billion

By The Discovery Wire

After 42 Years, Physicists Caught a Decay That Happens Once in 10 Billion

Some events in nature are so rare that catching one is less like running an experiment and more like winning a lottery you have entered every day for four decades. The LHCb experiment at CERN's Large Hadron Collider has now found the first evidence for one of them.

A decay predicted to almost never happen

LHCb studies particles containing beauty quarks — heavier relatives of the up and down quarks in ordinary matter. Beauty quarks are produced in huge numbers in LHC collisions, but each lives for only about one and a half trillionths of a second before decaying.

The new result concerns a decay known as Bd to mu mu, in which a particle containing a beauty quark decays into two muons. Theory predicts it happens only around once in every 10 billion beauty particles. Until now, no single experiment had found solid evidence for it.

The new measurement reached a statistical significance of 3.2 sigma — enough for physicists to call it the first evidence of the decay, but short of the five-sigma threshold particle physics requires to claim a discovery. More data will be needed, and the team says so plainly.

Why chase something this rare?

The rarer a decay is, the more sensitive it can be to the influence of new particles — particles too small or too heavy to produce directly, whose presence would subtly change how often the decay occurs. That is why physicists call this a golden channel for spotting physics beyond the Standard Model, our current best theory of fundamental particles and forces.

Measurements like this probe what researchers call the Zeptoverse — an unexplored realm at distances of a billionth of a trillionth of a meter, a zeptometer, where new particles and forces may be hiding. So far, the new measurement agrees with the Standard Model's predictions. Increasingly precise measurements are how physicists search for the first place the model begins to crack.

Professor Chris Parkes of the University of Manchester, who led the international LHCb collaboration through the construction of its upgrade, put the odds vividly: the chance of this decay happening is "roughly like going on a romantic date and, on your very first try, finding the one perfect match for you among everyone on Earth."

The instrument that made it possible

The result rests on the upgraded LHCb detector, a 15-year design and construction project by a 1,900-member international collaboration, which can now collect data at up to five times its previous rate. A key component is the Vertex Locator, a silicon pixel detector built in the U.K. that sits just five millimeters from the LHC beams and pinpoints where each particle was produced and where it decayed.

The upgraded experiment is expected to collect a much larger data set into the early 2030s, and a further planned upgrade could raise the data rate by another factor of ten — sharpening this measurement and others like it.

Forty-two years of searching produced, for now, evidence — honestly labeled, carefully measured, and pointing at a scale of nature no one has explored before. That is how the frontier actually moves.
