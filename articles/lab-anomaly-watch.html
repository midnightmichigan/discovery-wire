The Lab That Never Sleeps: Inside Our 20-Minute Anomaly Watch

We built ourselves a machine that never sleeps — and every twenty minutes, it takes the pulse of the sky.

This is one of the experiments running in our lab: an autonomous watch over three live, fully public data feeds. No private data, no secrets, no special access. Everything it reads is available to anyone with a web browser. Here's the plumbing.

Feed one: the sky overhead

Live aircraft positions across the continental United States, via the OpenSky Network — a community-run, open air-traffic database. Every few seconds it updates where thousands of aircraft are. The watch listens for the transponder squawk codes that mean something unusual is happening on board: 7500 (unlawful interference), 7600 (radio failure), 7700 (general emergency). Most of the time there are none. A clean sky is a data point.

Feed two: the nation's alert wire

The National Weather Service's active-alerts feed carries every official alert in the country — hundreds of them, mostly ordinary: small-craft advisories, flood watches, wind warnings. The watch scans for the rare types: 911 telephone outages, civil emergency messages, law-enforcement warnings, radiological hazards, shelter-in-place orders. On a typical pass, the rarest of the rare is nowhere to be found — and that absence is the story.

Feed three: the ground itself

The public USGS earthquake feed, last-24-hour window, magnitude 2.5 and up. The watch looks for shaking that doesn't fit — events whose human "felt report" counts are wildly out of proportion to their magnitude. A tremor nobody noticed is normal. A tremor everyone noticed is a note for the next pass.

What happens every twenty minutes

Pull all three feeds. Compare each reading against its baseline. Write an anomaly digest — a plain-English memo of what was seen, what was clean, and what deserves a second look on the next run. Track anything strange across runs: a blip that persists gets escalated; a blip that fades is just logged.

That's it. That's the experiment. Boring, relentless, honest.

What this is NOT

It is not a warning system, and it is not a prediction machine. We don't issue public alerts, and we don't forecast anything — nobody can. The lab's own charter says it plainly: observation, not warning. A telescope, not a siren.

We'll be sharing dispatches from the watch as it runs — the process, the weirdness of what counts as "normal" at 3 AM, and the occasional genuinely strange page in the log. No hype. Just the instruments, read out loud.
