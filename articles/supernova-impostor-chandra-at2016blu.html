The Supernova Impostor: Chandra Finds What Keeps Making a Giant Star Erupt Every 113 Days

For more than a decade, a giant star 30 million light-years away has kept doing something a star is not supposed to survive: erupting like a supernova, again and again.

The star is AT 2016blu, in the spiral galaxy NGC 4559. It is a luminous blue variable — a hot, massive, unstable star, at least 33 times the mass of the Sun — of a rare type that undergoes rapid, repeated brightenings so extreme they resemble supernova explosions. Unlike a real supernova, the star is not destroyed. Astronomers call it a "supernova impostor."

Between 2012 and 2026, astronomers detected at least 27 bursts of optical light from AT 2016blu, roughly every 113 days.

Caught in X-rays at the right moment

"For 15 years, astronomers have been watching this star get really bright and then dim again on a regular basis," said Mojgan Aghakhanloo of the University of Virginia, who led the study. "Until we were able to get Chandra data at the right time, we couldn't figure out what was making this happen."

When optical monitoring showed the star entering an outburst in early 2026, NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory was triggered under its Target of Opportunity program and observed in March 2026. Chandra data showed AT 2016blu was at least 100 times brighter in X-rays than in Chandra observations from 2001–2002, before the outbursts began.

This is the first X-ray detection of an erupting star of this class, and the first such object found with a black hole or neutron star companion. The paper was published in The Astrophysical Journal (Aghakhanloo et al., 2026, DOI: 10.3847/1538-4357/ae9a86).

A hidden companion on an oval orbit

The team's explanation: a previously hidden companion — either a stellar-mass black hole or a neutron star — orbits the giant star on an oval-shaped path. At closest approach, roughly every 113 days, material ejected by the giant star is captured by the companion, spirals in at very high speed, heats up, and shines brightly in X-rays. That high-energy blast in turn heats the dense gas around the system, which glows in visible light — the "eruption" telescopes have been recording since 2012.

AT 2016blu is bright enough in X-rays to qualify as a candidate ultraluminous X-ray source, a rare class whose members are thought to contain neutron stars or black holes. The result suggests a link between those sources and violent pre-supernova stellar eruptions — and a possible clue to why some massive stars change in brightness before they finally explode.

What remains unknown is the companion itself: black hole or neutron star, nobody can say yet. Chandra did not photograph it directly — at 30 million light-years, the pair cannot be separated that way. Its presence is inferred from the X-ray behaviour, timed to the outburst.
