The Disappearing Planet: A Hidden Neighbor Is Tilting TOI-2535 b Out of View

By The Discovery Wire

Illustration by The Discovery Wire

The Disappearing Planet: A Hidden Neighbor Is Tilting TOI-2535 b Out of View

A planet does not have to go anywhere to disappear. It just has to stop crossing our line of sight.

That is what happened to TOI-2535 b, a hot Jupiter about 1,000 light-years away that orbits its star in just 8.25 days — roughly ten times faster than Mercury orbits our Sun. UCLA-led astronomers watched it transit its star for seven years. Then, in 2026, the transits stopped.

Watched by TESS, 2019 to 2026

TOI-2535 b was identified in images from NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS), which finds planets by measuring the small dip in a star's brightness when a planet passes in front of it. Those transits only work with a precise alignment between star, planet, and our line of sight.

TESS returned to this patch of sky many times between April 2019 and March 2026. The clues built up slowly: the transits did not keep a regular schedule, running dramatically early or late by up to six hours, and they grew shorter and shallower over the years — until they failed to appear at all in 2026.

A wobble like a top

The team's explanation, published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters (Yee et al., 2026, DOI: 10.3847/2041-8213/aeb4a4): an unseen neighboring planet, on an orbit tilted relative to TOI-2535 b, is pulling on it. The two planets make each other's orbits wobble like a top, so each circles its sun obliquely — and as the wobble tilts TOI-2535 b out of alignment, its transits vanish from our view.

"We think of the universe as resembling clockwork, where everything is regular, but that's not true," said Samuel Yee, a 51 Pegasi b postdoctoral fellow at UCLA and the paper's corresponding author. "This is the most extreme example we've seen because the two planets are interacting so strongly. The planet's orbit is tilting so quickly that its transit disappeared while we were watching."

It is the same geometry that makes eclipses rare on Earth: because the Moon's orbit is tilted, it only blocks the Sun when everything lines up exactly.

When will it be back?

Not soon. The calculations say TOI-2535 b will next transit its star sometime in 30 to 50 years — though its hidden neighbor may come into view sooner.

The wobble also hints at how the system formed. Because TOI-2535 b has a close neighbor, Yee argues it probably migrated inward by slowly spiraling rather than being flung in by a violent encounter — a formation channel that may not happen very often.

The planet itself was characterized as an extremely hot, Jupiter-sized world using the transit size plus precise observations from the WIYN 3.5-meter telescope at Kitt Peak National Observatory in Arizona.
