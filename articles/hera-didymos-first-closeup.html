Hera Just Saw the Side of Didymos DART Never Photographed

By The Discovery Wire

Illustration by The Discovery Wire

Hera Just Saw the Side of Didymos DART Never Photographed

Four years ago, humanity deliberately crashed a spacecraft into an asteroid. On November 5, 2026, the follow-up inspector returned its first close look at the scene.

ESA's Hera spacecraft imaged the binary asteroid system of Didymos and its moonlet Dimorphos from about 60 kilometres away with its Asteroid Framing Camera. The frame shows the face of the 780-metre-wide Didymos that NASA's DART camera never photographed — a rugged, boulder-strewn hemisphere — with the 151-metre Dimorphos visible at the lower left, partly in shadow.

"A whole new world"

"The first thing we said was wow!" said Hera Principal Investigator Patrick Michel. "This is the other side of Didymos from the one that DART's camera showed us shortly before impact, so this feels like looking at a whole new world."

The equatorial ridge that girds Didymos appears on this side too, confirming it wraps the whole body. But the density of boulders surprised the team. One hypothesis already on the table: some of that debris was flung off Dimorphos by DART's 2022 impact and travelled the roughly one kilometre between the two bodies before settling on Didymos. "We have exciting work ahead of us to sort it out," said Hera Mission Scientist Michael Küppers.

Why this visit matters

On September 26, 2022, DART struck Dimorphos at 6.6 kilometres per second and shortened its orbit by about 33 minutes — the first demonstration that a kinetic impactor can deflect an asteroid. But DART's camera died in the impact, and Dimorphos's mass has never been measured directly, leaving the deflection's true efficiency uncertain. Hera will weigh Dimorphos, map the crater, and — with its Juventas and Milani CubeSats — probe the moonlet's interior with the first radar ever flown to an asteroid.

ESA plans to release the first detailed, full-resolution images of both asteroids on November 25, 2026. The system is currently about 196.8 million kilometres from Earth — an 11-minute one-way radio delay.
