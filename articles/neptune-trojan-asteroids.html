Neptune Was Hiding Its Trailing Trojans Behind the Milky Way

Beginning in 1906, telescope observations revealed a population of asteroids leading and trailing Jupiter, which came to be known as the Trojan and Greek populations. Since then, astronomers have found similar families around other planets — but Neptune's trailing cloud stayed elusive.

An international team led by researchers at Queen's University Belfast has now resolved that gap, in a paper published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters.

Found in Rubin's early data

The team analyzed data from the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in the Andes in Chile — an 8.4-meter telescope with the largest digital camera ever built, which began operations in June 2025 and is now conducting the Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST).

From Rubin's early test observations, the team identified two asteroids and a candidate as permanent members of Neptune's trailing (L5) Trojans — objects that share Neptune's orbit, trailing the planet.

Lead author Dr. Meg Schwamb of Queen's University Belfast said early Rubin data is already expanding knowledge of the solar system's small bodies, and that these observations provided the clearest view yet of Neptune's trailing Trojan cloud.

Why they stayed hidden for so long

These are distant, faint objects moving across the night sky. For the past 10 years, Neptune's Trojans have been crowded in a region of sky that placed them in front of the center of the Milky Way — an extremely dense, bright backdrop that obscured tiny foreground sources.

That backdrop is now shifting. Schwamb noted the trailing cloud has begun to move out of that region, and combined with Rubin's depth and sky coverage, the team was able to double the number of known objects in this population on long-lived orbits.

The Rubin images, taken through different filters, also showed that Neptune's leading and trailing Trojans have similar surfaces.

What comes next

Over the next nine years of the LSST survey, Rubin is expected to discover tens to hundreds of Neptune Trojans in both the leading and trailing populations, plus tens of thousands of icy bodies in the trans-Neptunian region and Kuiper Belt.

Two stable Trojans and a candidate are a small start. They are also a preview: a survey built to census the whole southern sky is already finding the solar system's hardest-to-see residents in its early data.
