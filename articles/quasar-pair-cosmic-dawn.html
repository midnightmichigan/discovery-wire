Two Monster Black Holes Were Already Shining in One Galaxy Less Than a Billion Years After the Big Bang

By The Discovery Wire

Illustration by The Discovery Wire

Two Monster Black Holes Were Already Shining in One Galaxy Less Than a Billion Years After the Big Bang

One giant black hole that early is hard to explain. This galaxy has two — both shining at once.

Astronomers have identified a pair of black-hole-powered quasars inside a single galaxy seen less than one billion years after the Big Bang — about 900 million years into the universe's history, when it was roughly a thirteenth of its current age.

The pair

The system, designated J1602+4228AB and nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, is the most distant compact quasar pair ever identified, and only the third known quasar pair from cosmic dawn. Its two members are separated by approximately 18,100 light-years — about one-fifth the diameter of the Milky Way.

The masses are the hard part. One black hole contains roughly 1.5 billion times the Sun's mass; its companion weighs about 400 million solar masses. As lead author Silvia Onorato of NSF NOIRLab put it, there is no definitive theoretical explanation for how even a single one of these giant quasars exists so early — much less two.

How it was found

The discovery emerged from the Aether survey, a James Webb Space Telescope program led by Emanuele Paolo Farina of NSF NOIRLab/Gemini, which examined 200 early quasars with Webb's Near-Infrared Spectrograph Integral Field Unit. One quasar, J1602+4228A, appeared to have a faint companion. Follow-up observations with the Gemini North telescope in Hawaiʻi confirmed the two sources could be distinguished individually despite their exceptionally close apparent separation — collected, Onorato noted, in only two nights under exceptionally clean Maunakea skies.

Why pairs are so rare

Astronomers expect pairs of active black holes to form as galaxies interact and merge, funneling gas toward their centers. But black holes shine as quasars for relatively short periods, so catching two neighbors in that luminous phase at the same time is difficult — and harder still in the distant universe, where faint objects separated by tiny angles are hard to resolve.

The team's suggestion, offered as a possibility rather than a confirmed account, is that this system sits in an exceptionally dense region of the early universe, where frequent galaxy interactions supplied gas for rapid star formation and black hole growth. Either way, it is a direct laboratory for how the first massive galaxies and their central black holes assembled together.

The nickname was created through the A Hua He Inoa program in collaboration with Hawaiian language and astronomy institutions.

Reference: Onorato et al., "Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua: A Close Quasar Pair at Cosmic Dawn in the Aether Survey," The Astrophysical Journal Letters, 10 November 2026. DOI: 10.3847/2041-8213/aeb11f. Source: SciTechDaily, Nov 17, 2026.
