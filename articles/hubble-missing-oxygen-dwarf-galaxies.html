The Case of the Missing Oxygen: Hubble Finds Dwarf Galaxies Keep Only a Tenth of What Their Stars Make

By The Discovery Wire

NASA, ESA, Hubble Heritage-ESA/Hubble Collaboration, Alessandra Aloisi (STScI, ESA)

The Case of the Missing Oxygen: Hubble Finds Dwarf Galaxies Keep Only a Tenth of What Their Stars Make

The stars did the work. The oxygen is mostly gone.

In a paper published Monday in The Astrophysical Journal, researchers using NASA's Hubble Space Telescope studied 37 nearby dwarf galaxies — small systems containing a few thousand to hundreds of millions of stars — and found that much of the oxygen they expected to see is missing. Simulations predict significantly more oxygen should reside in these galaxies. So where did it go?

Counting what the stars made, and what is left

Oxygen is a useful tracer: it is the most abundant element after hydrogen and helium, relatively easy to detect, and its production inside stars is well understood. In the Galaxies Losing Oxygen via Winds (GLOW) project, funded by NASA and the National Science Foundation, the team reconstructed each galaxy's star-formation history from Hubble observations of individual stars, then compared how much oxygen those stars should have produced with how much remains in the galaxies' stars, gas, and dust.

The galaxies retained between 4% and 25% of the oxygen their stars produced, with a mean of about 11%, according to the Sci.News summary of the census. Nearly all of the retained oxygen sits in the gas rather than in stars or dust.

Blown out by their own stars

"Oxygen is produced by stars, which are inside of galaxies. We know that the oxygen had to be inside of the galaxies at some point in the past, so the oxygen must have been ejected," said co-investigator Grace Telford, assistant professor in the Department of Physics and Astronomy at the University of Utah. "And to do that, you need energy from massive stars and supernova explosions to drive material out of the host galaxies and into the space between galaxies."

In other words, galactic winds unleashed by supernovae are apparently more effective at removing oxygen from low-mass galaxies than current models predict. Smaller galaxies, with weaker gravity, lose proportionally more.

The open question: where is it now?

Previous teams have measured some oxygen in the diffuse halo around dwarf galaxies — the circumgalactic medium — but it is still unclear whether most of the missing oxygen is sitting there, or whether it has been completely ejected into the space between galaxies. Earlier ultraviolet work accounts for only part of the remainder, so a large share may have left the galaxies' surroundings entirely.

That is the question the team wants to chase next. GLOW principal investigator Kristen Wingfield McQuinn — mission head of the Science Operations Center for NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope at the Space Telescope Science Institute, and an astronomer at Rutgers University — hopes to study even lower-mass galaxies, using Roman to find faint systems and Hubble and Webb to follow up. Telford's future work will use Hubble to zoom in on the individual massive stars that produce the oxygen and help eject it.

Dwarf galaxies are the most common galaxies in the universe, and they are considered building blocks of larger systems like our own. If the smallest galaxies routinely lose nine-tenths of what their stars forge, then the space between galaxies is being enriched far more efficiently than our models assumed — and the chemistry of the cosmos is, in part, written by what small galaxies could not hold on to.
