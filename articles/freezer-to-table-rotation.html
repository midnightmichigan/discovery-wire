Every household freezer has an archaeology layer: food bought with real money, frozen with good intentions, and discovered a year later under a crust of frost, unrecognizable and headed for the trash. That is not a storage problem. It is a rotation problem — nothing in the freezer has an order, so nothing in the freezer has a turn. A simple first-in, first-out system fixes it with labels, placement, and one weekly habit.

Rule one: nothing goes in without a name and a date

Freezer amnesia starts at the door. Unlabelled, everything frozen looks the same in week six. The standard is a strip of freezer tape or a label on every single item: what it is, and the date it went in. Not the date you bought it — the date it froze, because that is the clock that matters.

Freezer tape beats regular tape (it stays stuck at freezer temperatures) and beats permanent marker on the bag (bags get reused and cross-outs get confusing). Portion before freezing, too: a family-size block of ground meat is a commitment; four flat, labelled portions are four future dinners that thaw fast and cook faster. Flat-freezing in zip bags — press the air out, freeze flat on a sheet pan, then file upright like books — is the single biggest space saver in a small freezer.

Rule two: new food goes behind, old food comes forward

This is the whole rotation, and it is the step everyone skips. When groceries come home, the new items go to the back or bottom of the freezer and the existing stock moves forward. Whatever is at the front is what gets cooked this week. Grocery stores run entire businesses on this principle; a home freezer needs exactly the same discipline at a fraction of the scale.

Bins make it physical instead of mental. One bin per category — meats, vegetables, ready meals, bread and extras — so "what do we have?" is answered by pulling a bin, not by excavating. Clear bins, sized to the freezer, with the oldest items riding on top.

Rule three: one freezer meal a week, chosen from the front

Rotation only works if the front of the freezer actually gets eaten. Build one meal a week deliberately from the oldest stock — a "freezer night" — and let it be easy: the point is using what exists, not performing a recipe. This habit does three jobs at once. It keeps stock turning over before quality fades, it creates one no-shopping dinner every week, and it surfaces what is running low while there is still time to catch it on sale instead of at full price.

General freezer quality guidance worth knowing: frozen food kept continuously at 0°F stays safe indefinitely, but quality declines — most meats are at their best within roughly 3–12 months depending on the cut, vegetables around 8–12 months, and prepared meals around 2–3 months. Rotation is about eating food while it is still good, not racing a safety deadline.

Keep the list where the food is

A rotation system dies if the inventory lives in someone's memory. A magnetic dry-erase board on the freezer door — item, date in, quantity — updated with a marker kept right there, is the low-tech version that works. Cross off as things come out.
