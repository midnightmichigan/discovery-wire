After Sunset on Mars, the Clouds Drift Through Gale Crater

By The Discovery Wire

NASA/JPL-Caltech (PIA26805)

After Sunset on Mars, the Clouds Drift Through Gale Crater

The Sun had already set. The clouds were still glowing.

NASA's Curiosity Mars rover captured drifting clouds on Oct. 31, 2026, the 5,061st Martian day, or sol, of its mission. The rover used its navigation cameras to record post-sunset clouds — called twilight clouds — over Gale Crater.

A short season, every fall

These clouds appear for only a short time each fall over Gale Crater, the crater Curiosity has been exploring for more than 14 years. That long baseline is part of what makes a simple cloud image valuable: the same rover, in the same place, watching the same sky change with the seasons, year after year.

Twilight clouds are visible after sunset because they sit high enough to still catch sunlight while the ground below has gone dark — the same basic geometry that produces noctilucent, or night-shining, clouds here on Earth.

Fourteen years of weather

Curiosity was built by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, which leads the mission for NASA's Science Mission Directorate as part of the Mars Exploration Program. Fourteen years into a mission that was never expected to last this long, it is still doing the quiet work: taking the weather's picture, one sol at a time.
