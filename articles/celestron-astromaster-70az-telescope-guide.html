The Step Up From Binoculars: Celestron AstroMaster 70AZ, Honestly

Our SkyMaster 15x70 guide makes the case for binoculars first. Fair enough — but some of you already own binoculars, and some of you are buying for a kid who has been asking for "a real telescope" by name. The beginner telescope that has answered that question for the better part of two decades is the Celestron AstroMaster 70AZ (model 21061).

Full honesty up front: this is a specs-and-track-record guide, not a hands-on lab test. We have not unboxed one for this article. What we can do is read the manufacturer's numbers carefully and tell you where this scope's reputation is earned and where it isn't.

What it is

  • A 70mm refractor with fully coated glass optics, per Celestron's specifications — a 700mm focal length (f/10) achromatic tube, the classic long, skinny beginner refractor shape.
  • An altazimuth mount on an adjustable-height tripod — it moves the way you'd expect (up-down, left-right), with a panhandle-style control, instead of the equatorial mounts that confuse first-timers. The tripod is included this time, unlike our binoculars pick.
  • Two eyepieces and a bonus astronomy software package in the standard kit, so the box is genuinely complete on night one.
  • A terrestrial bonus: a refractor on an alt-az mount with an erect-image diagonal doubles as a daytime spotting scope for birds, boats, and distant ridgelines, which is more than most beginner telescopes can say.

Through a 70mm f/10, the Moon is the show — craters along the terminator have real texture. Saturn's rings separate from the planet. Jupiter shows its two dark cloud bands and the four Galilean moons, the same moons our binocular readers spot, now with the planet itself resolved into a disc.

What it isn't

  • It is not a deep-sky instrument. Galaxies and most nebulae stay faint smudges in 70mm of aperture under anything but dark skies. This is a Moon-and-planets scope, and it is good at exactly that.
  • It is not a photography rig. No tracking motor, no camera mount worth the name. Phone-through-the-eyepiece snapshots of the Moon, yes; astrophotography, no.
  • It is not "600x power," whatever marketplace listings shout. Usable magnification tops out around 140–165x on the best nights; the honest working range is 35x–100x. Any beginner telescope advertised on magnification alone is telling on itself.

Our verdict

The order of operations from our binoculars guide stands: binoculars first for most people. But if a telescope is the gift or the goal, the AstroMaster 70AZ is the budget refractor with the longest track record of actually satisfying beginners — a complete kit, a mount a ten-year-old can run, and optics pointed at the two targets beginners actually look at: the Moon and the planets.
