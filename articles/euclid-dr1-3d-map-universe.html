Euclid Just Handed Us the First Draft of the Universe's 3D Map

Nine hundred million galaxies. That is not a typo — that is one line in the inventory of a single data release.

On November 12, 2026, the Euclid Consortium and the European Space Agency (ESA) released DR1 Foundation, the first major batch of data from the Euclid space telescope since its launch in mid-2023. The release covers some 13 months of observations, an area of sky equivalent to 9,500 full moons, and captures light from more than a billion objects. ESA calls it the largest map of the sky of its kind ever produced from space.

"These are far more than beautiful pictures; today marks a scientific milestone in our study of the cosmos," said ESA's director of science, Carole Mundell.

What is actually in the release

Analysis of about 10,000 science images from DR1 Foundation turns up numbers that are hard to hold in your head:

  • More than 900 million galaxies
  • More than 60 million quasar candidates — the brilliant cores of distant galaxies
  • Around 15,000 strong gravitational lensing candidates — the largest catalog of strong lenses ever assembled, larger than all previous catalogs combined
  • Spectra from more than 160 million sources
  • Euclid Wide Survey results from 1,900 square degrees of sky — about 14% of the area planned for the six-year mission

"This is a change in scale from Euclid's previous releases, which included a few days of observations over around 60 deg² of sky," said Euclid project scientist Valeria Pettorino at ESA. "We're now releasing more than a year of data covering a significantly larger area. This unlocks groundbreaking science."

Why a map, and why 3D?

A flat picture of the sky tells you where things appear. A 3D map tells you where things are. By splitting galaxy light into spectra — rainbows that reveal each galaxy's distance — scientists can place hundreds of millions of galaxies inside the universe's actual structure: the vast, invisible mesh called the cosmic web.

That structure is the real target. Euclid was built to attack the dark universe — dark matter, dark energy, and the nearly 30-year-old mystery of why the expansion of the universe is accelerating. Strong gravitational lenses help too: when a foreground galaxy bends the light of a more distant one, astronomers can effectively "weigh" the foreground galaxy, including its invisible dark matter.

Just the beginning

As large as DR1 Foundation is, it covers only about 14% of the sky Euclid will survey. The full Data Release 1 is due in mid-2027, and will add weak-lensing observations and calculations of how galaxies and galaxy clusters fit into the cosmic web.

We find the honest framing refreshing: this is not the finished map. It is the foundation — the first draft of an atlas of everything, drawn so we can finally ask the dark universe some direct questions.
