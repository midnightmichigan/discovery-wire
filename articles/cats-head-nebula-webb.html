Something Is Watching From 7,500 Light-Years Away — Webb and Hubble Catch the Cat's Head Nebula Staring Back

By The Discovery Wire

NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI; Image Processing: Joseph DePasquale (STScI)

Something Is Watching From 7,500 Light-Years Away

Just in time for Halloween, NASA released a new look at one of the sky's strangest silhouettes: the Cat's Head Nebula, a dusty pillar inside the much larger Carina Nebula, about 7,500 light-years from Earth.

The "cat's head" shape was likely sculpted over time by the intense ultraviolet radiation and winds from massive stars in the Carina Nebula. As that stellar feedback erodes the surrounding gas and dust, the denser material inside the feline-shaped pillar resists erosion — at least for now, as NASA puts it.

Three telescopes' worth of eyes

  • Hubble (visible light): the pillar looks opaque, wrapped in a luminous ring of gas. A glowing red spot — the cat's "eye" — is likely an infant star still embedded in the dust and gas it formed from.
  • Webb's NIRCam (near-infrared): the filament turns more transparent, revealing finer texture plus a crowd of background stars and galaxies. The eye shifts to yellow.
  • Webb's MIRI (mid-infrared): warm dust and carbon-based molecules light up, and a bright yellow source just to the right of the pillar is probably a young star shining through the dusty red environment.

Why the different views matter

Infant stars are heavily obscured in visible light because of all the dust around them. In infrared, they stand out. Comparing the same structure across wavelengths tells astronomers about the gaseous, dusty, and stellar makeup of the nebula at the same time — a fuller picture of how stars are born inside pillars like this one.

The Cat's Head is still forming stars. The cat, it turns out, is not just watching us — it's busy making suns.
