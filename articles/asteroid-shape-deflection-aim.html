To Deflect an Asteroid, Where You Aim Matters More Than Anyone Thought

If a dangerous asteroid were ever found on a collision course with Earth, one last-resort defense would be a nuclear detonation near its surface. The burst of X-rays would vaporize one side of the rock, and the recoil from that escaping vapor would nudge the asteroid onto a new path. Simple in outline — but new simulations show the details are anything but simple.

Spherical asteroids don't exist

"Most researchers were modeling asteroids like a sphere. It makes the math easier. It's a huge calculation, and the codes can handle spheres. The trouble is, spherical asteroids don't exist," said Nathan Moore, a physicist at Sandia National Laboratories who led the research, published in the journal Icarus.

To fix that, Moore's team built what they describe as the most accurate asteroid-deflection model to date. Earlier models had to choose between realistic shapes and high-fidelity math. The new approach combines two Sandia simulation codes — ALEGRA, used for fast, high-energy experiments, and SPARTA, a direct-simulation Monte Carlo code used for weak, slowly evolving forces — and runs, by the team's account, over 1,600 times faster than the old models. That speed lets researchers start from high-resolution 3D imagery of real asteroids gathered by spacecraft flybys, then simulate an X-ray burst at a chosen size, distance, and side.

Same rock, different side, five times the push

Irradiating different sides of the same simulated asteroid changed both the momentum imparted and the spin induced. In one case, the deflection velocity — the change in the asteroid's speed after the burst — was 3.9 millimeters per second from one angle but only 0.8 millimeters per second from another: a nearly fivefold difference from aim alone. For a different asteroid, the change in rotational speed after deflection varied by a factor of 17.

"We expected aim to matter," Moore said. "What surprised us was how much."

Push, don't smash

The goal of any deflection is to push the asteroid, not shatter it into smaller but still dangerous pieces. The model currently handles breakup of rocks on the surface; predicting breakup of an entire asteroid body will take more research. It also needs accurate shape data from all sides of the object — information humanity might not have time to gather in a real emergency, and positioning a device at the ideal distance from the correct side of a tumbling rock is its own challenge.

Still, the direction is clear. As Moore put it: "Pick the right side and you win. Guess wrong and it could be a bad day."
