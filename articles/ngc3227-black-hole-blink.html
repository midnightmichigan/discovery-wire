A Black Hole Blinked: A Gas Cloud Dimmed NGC 3227's Glow for Weeks

By The Discovery Wire

Illustration by The Discovery Wire

Some galaxies are powered by actively feeding black holes, and occasionally one of them changes its appearance so fast that astronomers give the event its own name: a "changing-look" event. The galaxy brightens or dims, and spectral features appear or disappear, on timescales of weeks to months.

A team led by Kaiwen Zhang of the University of Wyoming has now caught one of the clearest examples yet, in the Seyfert galaxy NGC 3227, about 77 million light-years away, which hosts a black hole of roughly 10 million solar masses.

The disappearing act

Monitoring the galaxy's visible light at the Wyoming Infrared Observatory, the team watched its optical brightness drop by about 70% over a few weeks. At the same time, the broad hydrogen-beta emission line — light from fast-moving gas close to the black hole, lit up by the black hole's central glow — dropped by about 80%, nearly disappearing.

Both recovered within about two months.

Changing-look events usually turn out to be changes in how fast the black hole is feeding. This one, the team argues, was different. Comparing the event against the galaxy's normal variability, they conclude a feeding fluctuation is unlikely to explain so dramatic a decline. Instead, the evidence points to a cloud sitting closer to the black hole than the line-emitting gas — a cloud that dimmed the central glow before that light could reach the gas, and attenuated the light along our line of sight as well. X-ray hardening observed at the onset of the decline supports the obscuration picture, and earlier X-ray changes in 2019 had already hinted at new gas moving in front of this black hole.

How thick a cloud?

Using simulations of how light passes through gas, the team estimated what it would take to dim visible light by 70%: an extremely dense, fully ionized, "Compton-thick" cloud, with a column density of about 1.5 × 10²⁴ atoms per square centimeter — 10 to 100 times higher than the X-ray-based estimate, a gap the team attributes to the X-ray observations ending before the deepest part of the optical dimming.

What the cloud actually is remains an open question. The team notes it could be a wind blown off the black hole's accretion disk, which simulations suggest can become very thick — but the present data cannot settle it.

NGC 3227 is, to date, the only clear example of a galaxy where a blocking cloud drove both the optical dimming and the near-disappearance of the broad emission lines. More data will be needed to confirm whether the same cloud caused both the X-ray and optical changes — but the event already shows that material very close to a black hole can dramatically reshape what we see from it, in a matter of weeks.

Source: Phys.org, Nov 23, 2026. Paper: Zhang et al., The Astrophysical Journal Letters (2026), DOI 10.3847/2041-8213/aeb6c3.
